# Senior Business Analyst — Advanced Interview Preparation

## Strategic Business Analysis

### The Senior BA vs Junior BA
The Senior BA does not just gather and document requirements — they shape strategy, lead stakeholders, and drive organisational change. The shift is from execution to leadership.

**Senior BA Competency Framework:**
| Dimension | Junior BA | Senior BA |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Scope | Project-level | Programme/Enterprise-level |
| Stakeholders | Team + department | C-suite, cross-functional, external |
| Requirements | Elicit and document | Challenge, negotiate, prioritise strategically |
| Problem Solving | Analyse defined problems | Identify and frame problems that weren't visible |
| Delivery | Follow process | Design and improve processes |
| Influence | Inform decisions | Drive decisions |
| Ambiguity | Needs guidance | Thrives in ambiguity |
| Domain Knowledge | Learning | Expert — trusted advisor |

**Key Senior BA Responsibilities:**
- Leading business analysis on complex, multi-workstream programmes
- Mentoring and reviewing junior BA work
- Defining BA standards, templates, and practices for the team
- Stakeholder strategy — identifying and managing political dynamics
- Business case development and benefit realisation
- Strategic alignment — ensuring solutions support corporate objectives
- Change management — leading the people side of transformation
- Pre-sales and discovery — supporting business development in consulting

### Enterprise Analysis

**Enterprise Analysis** is the highest-level BA activity — understanding the organisation's current capabilities and identifying what changes are needed to achieve strategic goals.

**Enterprise Analysis Activities:**
1. **Define the Business Need** — What problem are we solving? What opportunity are we pursuing?
2. **Assess Current Capability** — Capability mapping, gap analysis, maturity assessment
3. **Determine Solution Approach** — Build, buy, outsource, process change only?
4. **Define Solution Scope** — What's included and why?
5. **Develop the Business Case** — Investment justification, ROI, risk
6. **Prepare the Decision Package** — For executive sign-off

**Capability Mapping:**
Map what the organisation can currently do against what it needs to do:
- **Current Capabilities**: What processes, systems, people, data do we have?
- **Target Capabilities**: What do we need to achieve the strategy?
- **Gaps**: What's missing or insufficient?
- **Initiatives**: What projects/programmes close each gap?

### Business Case Development

The Senior BA is often the primary author or key contributor to business cases.

**Business Case Structure:**
1. **Executive Summary** — 1 page; decision needed, recommended option, key financials
2. **Strategic Context** — Why now? How does this align to corporate strategy?
3. **Problem Statement / Opportunity** — What are we solving?
4. **Options Analysis** — Minimum 3 options: do nothing, minimum viable, full solution
5. **Recommended Option** — With rationale
6. **Financial Analysis**
   - Costs: Capex, Opex, one-time vs recurring
   - Benefits: Tangible (revenue increase, cost reduction) and Intangible (brand, compliance)
   - ROI, NPV, Payback Period
7. **Risk Assessment** — Key risks and mitigations
8. **Delivery Approach** — Timeline, resources, approach
9. **Success Metrics** — How will we know it worked?
10. **Recommendation and Next Steps**

**Financial Metrics BAs Must Know:**
```
ROI = (Net Benefit / Total Cost) × 100
     = ((Benefits - Costs) / Costs) × 100

Payback Period = Total Investment / Annual Net Benefit (years)

NPV = Sum of (Cash Flow / (1 + Discount Rate)^Year) - Initial Investment
      Positive NPV = investment creates value

Break-even Point = Fixed Costs / (Revenue per unit - Variable Cost per unit)
```

**Example Business Case Snippet:**
```
Project: Customer Self-Service Portal

Current State: 85% of customer queries handled by call centre (2,400 agents)
Average cost per call: ₹45 | Annual call volume: 12M calls | Annual cost: ₹540M

Target State: Self-service portal handles 40% of queries
Remaining calls handled more efficiently

Year 1 Investment: ₹42M (development, infrastructure, training)
Year 2-4 Savings: ₹80M per year (32M calls deflected × ₹12 net saving per deflected call)
Net 3-Year Benefit: ₹198M
ROI: 371% | Payback: 6.3 months
```

### Benefit Realisation Management

One of the most overlooked BA activities — ensuring the promised benefits actually materialise.

**Benefit Realisation Framework:**
1. **Define Benefits** — Tangible and intangible, with measurement method
2. **Assign Benefit Owners** — Business owners accountable for realising each benefit
3. **Set Baselines** — Measure current state before the change
4. **Define Triggers** — When should each benefit be measured?
5. **Track and Report** — Regular benefit tracking through and after project delivery
6. **Course Correct** — If benefits aren't materialising, identify why and intervene

**Benefit Types:**
- **Financial**: Revenue increase, cost reduction, cost avoidance
- **Efficiency**: Time saved, error reduction, faster processing
- **Quality**: Customer satisfaction, defect reduction, compliance
- **Strategic**: Market share, brand value, innovation capability

## Advanced Stakeholder Management

### Political Dynamics and Stakeholder Strategy

Senior BAs must navigate organisational politics — not to play games, but to get the right outcomes.

**Stakeholder Analysis Framework:**
```
Power vs Interest Grid:
                     HIGH INTEREST
                         |
          Monitor        |    Manage Closely
LOW POWER ——————————————+——————————————— HIGH POWER
          Keep Informed  |    Keep Satisfied
                         |
                     LOW INTEREST
```

**Manage Closely (High Power, High Interest):**
- These stakeholders can make or break the project
- Regular one-on-ones, early involvement in decisions
- Understand their personal objectives (career, legacy, metrics they're measured on)

**Keep Satisfied (High Power, Low Interest):**
- They can veto but don't care about details
- Brief, executive-level updates
- Alert them early if something affects their area
- Never surprise them in a steering committee

**Show Consideration (Low Power, High Interest):**
- They feel the impact but can't influence directly
- Keep them informed, use them as validators
- Ignoring them breeds resentment; they talk

**Monitor (Low Power, Low Interest):**
- Periodic updates only; don't over-invest

**Stakeholder Influence Mapping:**
Beyond formal power, map informal influence:
- Who do the decision-makers listen to?
- Whose opinion carries disproportionate weight?
- Who are the resistors and why?
- Who can be coalition builders?

### Difficult Stakeholder Management

**The Executive Sponsor Who Isn't Engaged:**
- Request a standing 30-minute monthly checkpoint
- Frame it as risk management: "I need your input to protect the delivery date"
- Send a one-page status brief before each meeting
- Make it easy for them to say yes: bring options, not problems

**The Resistor:**
- Never fight resistance publicly — you will lose
- Understand their concern (threat to their territory? fear of change? past bad experiences?)
- Involve them in design: "I want your expertise on this" turns critics into owners
- Share early wins that address their specific concern
- If genuine sabotage, escalate to sponsor privately with evidence

**The Sponsor Who Keeps Changing Their Mind:**
- Document decisions formally with email confirmation every time
- Create a decision log visible to all senior stakeholders
- Bring the sponsor's previous decision to each meeting: "Last time we agreed X — do you want to revisit or proceed?"
- Quantify the cost of changes: "This change will push delivery 3 weeks and cost ₹8M"

**Conflicting Executive Priorities:**
- Surface the conflict explicitly — never try to hide it
- Facilitate a session between the two executives to resolve
- If unresolved, escalate to the common sponsor (CEO/MD) with a clear decision paper
- Document whatever outcome results

### Facilitation and Workshop Leadership

Senior BAs run high-stakes workshops with senior audiences.

**Workshop Design Principles:**
1. **Define the outcome, not the agenda** — "By the end, we will have signed off on the target operating model" not "9am: Introductions, 9:15 Opening presentation"
2. **Right people in the room** — A workshop with the wrong people wastes everyone's time
3. **Pre-read required** — Senior stakeholders will not sit through context-setting; send material in advance
4. **Time-box everything** — If it can't be resolved in the allotted time, park it and decide who resolves it offline
5. **Visible decision-making** — Write decisions on a whiteboard or shared screen as they're made

**Virtual Workshop Facilitation:**
- Smaller groups (max 12) work better virtually than in person
- Use polls and shared boards (Miro, Mural) for engagement
- Assign a co-facilitator to manage chat and tech issues
- Build in more breaks — 90 minutes maximum before a break virtually

**Workshop Failure Recovery:**
When a workshop derails:
- Acknowledge it: "We've gone a bit off track — let me refocus us on the outcome we need"
- Re-state the objective
- Time-box the current discussion: "Let's give this 5 more minutes then park it"
- Use breakout groups to parallel-process if the full group is stuck

## Advanced Requirements Engineering

### Requirement Quality Gates

Senior BAs establish standards for what constitutes a "ready" requirement.

**Definition of Ready (DoR) for User Stories:**
- [ ] Business value clearly stated (the "so that" clause is specific)
- [ ] Acceptance criteria in Given-When-Then format (min 3 scenarios)
- [ ] Happy path and at least 2 alternate/exception paths defined
- [ ] Non-functional requirements referenced (performance, security, accessibility)
- [ ] Dependencies identified
- [ ] Data requirements specified (input, output, validation rules)
- [ ] UI wireframe or mockup attached (for UI stories)
- [ ] Story is independently deliverable
- [ ] Team has estimated the story

**Requirements Traceability Matrix (RTM):**
Traces every requirement through to test cases:
```
BR-001 → FR-003, FR-004, FR-009 → TC-012, TC-013, TC-014
BR-002 → FR-005 → TC-015, TC-016
BR-003 → FR-006, FR-007 → TC-017
```
The RTM ensures: every business requirement has functional coverage, every functional requirement is tested, no "orphan" requirements, no "orphan" test cases.

### Handling Ambiguity at Scale

**The Ambiguity Framework:**
When requirements are genuinely unclear:
1. **Identify the ambiguity type**: Is it unclear because stakeholders disagree? Because it's genuinely unknown? Because it's a complex decision?
2. **Don't resolve it alone**: Ambiguity about business requirements is a business decision. The BA facilitates, not decides.
3. **Document the question**: "Decision Required: How should the system handle existing accounts when a customer merges two accounts? Options: A, B, C."
4. **Time-box the resolution**: Set a date by which the decision must be made, with the impact of delay stated.

**Prototyping to Resolve Ambiguity:**
- For UI requirements: Figma prototype in 2 hours resolves days of debate
- For business rules: A decision table or flowchart makes options visible
- For data requirements: A sample data model or entity diagram clarifies more than words

### Non-Functional Requirements Mastery

Senior BAs go deep on NFRs — often the area most junior BAs skip.

**Performance Requirements:**
```
NFR-001: The portal SHALL respond to any user request within 2 seconds (95th percentile)
         under a load of 5,000 concurrent users.

NFR-002: The batch processing job SHALL complete within the 2-hour maintenance window
         (11pm-1am) when processing up to 500,000 records.

NFR-003: The system SHALL handle a peak load of 10× the average transaction volume
         during month-end processing without performance degradation.
```

**Security Requirements:**
```
NFR-010: All customer data SHALL be encrypted at rest using AES-256.
NFR-011: All API calls SHALL require a valid JWT token with a maximum expiry of 1 hour.
NFR-012: The system SHALL lock any account after 5 consecutive failed login attempts
         for a minimum of 15 minutes.
NFR-013: All personally identifiable information SHALL be masked in system logs.
NFR-014: The system SHALL comply with GDPR Article 17 (right to erasure).
```

**Availability and Disaster Recovery:**
```
NFR-020: The system SHALL achieve 99.9% uptime (maximum 8.7 hours downtime per year).
NFR-021: Recovery Time Objective (RTO): System SHALL be restored within 4 hours of
         a critical failure.
NFR-022: Recovery Point Objective (RPO): No more than 1 hour of data SHALL be lost
         in the event of a disaster.
```

## Change Management for BAs

### The BA's Role in Change Management

Delivering requirements is not enough — the solution must be adopted. Senior BAs lead the people side of change.

**ADKAR Change Model:**
- **A**wareness — People know WHY the change is happening
- **D**esire — People WANT to change
- **K**nowledge — People know HOW to change
- **A**bility — People CAN change (training, tools, support)
- **R**einforcement — Change is sustained (metrics, recognition, accountability)

**BA's Contribution to Each ADKAR Phase:**
| Phase | BA Contribution |
|-------|----------------|
| Awareness | Stakeholder comms, leadership briefings |
| Desire | Benefit articulation, early wins, user involvement in design |
| Knowledge | Training content, user guides, FAQs, release notes |
| Ability | UAT, pilot, super-user programme, go-live support |
| Reinforcement | Benefits tracking, post-go-live review, issue resolution |

**Resistance Management:**
Resistance is normal — it means people care. The BA's job is to convert resistance from a blocker to a signal:
- Listen genuinely to concerns
- Acknowledge what's valid (not everything changes for the better)
- Involve resistors in finding solutions to their concerns
- Address concerns in design (build features that mitigate the pain)
- Be honest about what can and cannot be changed

### Training and Communication Strategy

**Communication Plan Elements:**
| Message | Audience | Channel | Frequency | Owner |
|---------|----------|---------|-----------|-------|
| Project launch announcement | All staff | Email + Town Hall | Once | Sponsor |
| Monthly project updates | Affected teams | Newsletter | Monthly | PM + BA |
| Training schedule | End users | Email | 4 weeks before go-live | BA |
| Go-live readiness | End users | Email + Briefing | 1 week before | BA |
| Go-live day instructions | End users | Email + Quick Reference | Go-live day | BA |

**Training Design Principles:**
- Role-based training — train people on what they will actually use
- Scenario-based: real tasks from their daily work, not feature walkthroughs
- Just-in-time: schedule training close to go-live so people remember
- Super-user network: identify champions in each team who provide first-line support
- Quick reference guides: 1-2 page visual summary for the most common tasks

## Senior BA Interview Preparation

### Strategic and Leadership Questions

**Q1. How do you align a project to corporate strategy?**
First, understand the strategy. Read the company's annual report, strategic plan, and any published OKRs/KPIs. Then map the project's benefits to strategic objectives — create an explicit traceability from "project delivers X" to "which strategic goal does X advance?" Present this alignment to the sponsor. If you can't draw a clear line from the project to strategy, that's a signal the project may not be the right investment. In practice, I would create a one-page "strategic alignment statement" for each programme that shows this linkage — it becomes the North Star when scope decisions get difficult.

**Q2. How do you mentor a junior BA?**
Mentoring is most effective when it's experiential, not instructional. I assign junior BAs to real tasks with increasing complexity — start with a focused requirements interview, then a workshop co-facilitation, then lead a section of a BRD, then own a full module. I review their work with specific, actionable feedback, not just corrections. I explain the "why" behind best practice, not just the "what." I create safe opportunities to fail and learn: better to make a mistake in a low-stakes workshop than in a steering committee. I also share what I've got wrong — it builds trust and shows that skills are earned, not innate.

**Q3. How do you handle a failing project you've been brought into?**
Diagnose before prescribing. In the first two weeks: read everything, meet everyone, listen. Map the gap between what was promised and what's been delivered. Identify the root causes — unclear requirements? Scope creep? Weak sponsorship? Technical debt? Then triage: what can be fixed, what must be deferred, what needs to be escalated? Present findings transparently to the sponsor — not to assign blame, but to reset expectations and get a realistic plan in place. The hardest part is often delivering bad news (the original timeline is not achievable), but it's far better to do it early with a credible plan than late with a crisis.

**Q4. Describe a time you challenged a senior stakeholder and how you handled it.**
Frame your answer with: situation, what you observed, how you prepared, how you delivered the challenge, the outcome, and what you learned. Good structure: "In a digital transformation at [type of company], the CTO proposed [approach]. My analysis showed [data-backed concern]. Rather than disagreeing in the steering committee, I requested a private meeting and presented my findings as questions: 'I want to make sure I understand this correctly — if we do X, the risk is Y. Is that a risk we've assessed?' This allowed him to reconsider without losing face. He revised the approach and later acknowledged the concern was valid." Key: challenge privately before publicly. Use data. Make it about the objective, not who's right.

**Q5. How do you manage requirements at programme level when multiple projects are interrelated?**
Programme-level requirements management requires: a shared requirements repository (not separate BRD per project), an enterprise data model or canonical data dictionary, cross-project dependency mapping (when Project A's output is Project B's input), a programme-level change control process, and a programme BA who owns the "integration layer" requirements. I would also establish a cross-project requirements review board that meets fortnightly — one BA from each project — to surface conflicts and gaps before they become delivery issues.

**Q6. How do you ensure requirements are testable?**
Every requirement must have an explicit, measurable acceptance criterion. I use four tests:
1. **Binary test**: Can a tester definitively say pass or fail? If not, rewrite.
2. **Data test**: Is the data needed to test it available? If not, define test data.
3. **Edge case test**: Have we defined what happens at the boundaries and exceptions?
4. **Regression test**: If this requirement changes, which tests would need updating?
If a requirement can't be tested, it can't be verified — which means we don't know if it was delivered.

### Advanced Scenario Questions

**Scenario 1: The Sponsor Wants to Cut Scope to Meet the Deadline**

*"You're 70% through a major CRM implementation. The sponsor says delivery must happen by end of Q3 (6 weeks away) or it misses the strategic window. The remaining scope will take 12 weeks. What do you do?"*

**Answer:**
Don't react — analyse first. Within 48 hours, produce a scope segmentation:
- **Tier 1 (Must Go-Live)**: Features needed for any users to use the system at all
- **Tier 2 (Should Go-Live)**: High-impact features, but system works without them
- **Tier 3 (Can Defer)**: Nice-to-have, can be Phase 2

Present the analysis to the sponsor and project manager with specific options:
- **Option A**: Go-live Tier 1 only (6 weeks) — what capabilities will and won't be available?
- **Option B**: Go-live Tier 1 + 2 (8 weeks) — 2-week delay but significantly more value
- **Option C**: Parallel workstreams — can additional resources be brought in to accelerate?

Quantify the impact of each option in business terms, not just features. Ensure the sponsor makes an informed choice with documented acceptance of the scope reduction and its business impact. Critically, do NOT simply agree to "do what we can" without clarity — that leads to go-live chaos.

**Scenario 2: Discovering a Legal/Compliance Gap Late in the Project**

*"During UAT, your team discovers that the solution as designed doesn't comply with GDPR — specifically, the data retention policy stores customer PII for 10 years but GDPR requires the ability to delete on request within 30 days. This wasn't in the original requirements. What happens?"*

**Answer:**
Escalate immediately — this is not a scope discussion, it's a legal risk. Same day: brief the PM, Legal/Compliance lead, and project sponsor. Do not suppress or delay this. Then: scope the technical gap — what does it take to implement data deletion capability? Is it a database change, an API addition, a UI feature? Get a rapid estimate. Present options: (a) build the capability before go-live — impact on timeline and cost; (b) delay go-live until compliant — impact on business; (c) go-live with a manual process (legal must review and accept this as temporary). Conduct a root cause analysis — why wasn't this in scope? Was a compliance SME involved in requirements? Implement process changes to prevent recurrence (compliance checklist in requirements templates). Document everything — paper trail is critical when legal risks are involved.

**Scenario 3: You're the BA on a Programme That's Being Cancelled**

*"Midway through a 12-month digital transformation, the board has decided to cancel the programme. As Senior BA, you've built strong relationships with the team and stakeholders. What do you do?"*

**Answer:**
Prioritise two things: people and knowledge.

**People first**: Be transparent with the team as soon as you're authorised to communicate. Don't leave them to find out through the grapevine. Acknowledge the personal impact. Help team members update their CVs and LinkedIn with the work they've done. Provide honest references.

**Knowledge second**: Lead a structured shutdown and knowledge transfer:
- Document what was delivered and what was in progress
- Archive all requirements artefacts in an accessible location with a clear index
- Write a programme closure report: what we learned, what worked, what didn't, recommendations if the initiative is ever resumed
- Ensure any dependencies on this programme are communicated to affected teams

**Third, understand "why"**: Was it budget? Strategy shift? Delivery failure? Understanding the cause informs lessons learned. Avoid attributing blame. A cancelled programme isn't a failed BA — programmes are cancelled for many reasons. The quality of your closure work is the last impression you leave.

**Scenario 4: Stakeholder Group Refuses to Use the New System**

*"Six months after go-live, adoption is at 15% against a target of 80%. The operations team is still using the old system (which runs in parallel). What do you do?"*

**Answer:**
Adoption failure is a change management failure, not just a system problem. Investigate before acting:
- **User research**: Interview a sample of non-adopters. Why aren't they using it? (Complexity? Missing features? Habit? Fear of job impact?)
- **Usage analytics**: Where do users drop off? What features are being used vs ignored?
- **Line manager engagement**: Are managers reinforcing the change or quietly condoning the old system?
- **Technical barriers**: Are there login issues, performance problems, missing functionality?

Based on findings, design targeted interventions:
- If it's usability: targeted training, UX improvements, super-user embedded support
- If it's missing features: rapid prioritisation of blockers into next sprint
- If it's resistance: leadership visibility, escalation to manager level, WIIFM messaging ("What's In It For Me")
- If it's habit: time-box the parallel run and set a hard cutover date with executive support

Critically, escalate to the sponsor — without executive reinforcement, adoption programmes fail. The sponsor must visibly use the system and communicate the expectation.

**Scenario 5: Highly Ambitious Project with Unclear Scope**

*"You've been brought in as Senior BA for a 'digital transformation' initiative. There's a ₹50 crore budget, an 18-month timeline, a vague brief ('transform the customer experience'), and a steering committee that can't agree on priorities. Where do you start?"*

**Answer:**
The biggest risk here is starting development on the wrong thing. The first 4-6 weeks must be discovery and strategy alignment — not requirements.

**Week 1-2: Stakeholder Landscape**
- Map all stakeholders: who are the key voices? Who has budget authority? Who has veto power?
- Conduct 30-minute structured interviews with each steering committee member: "What does success look like in 18 months?"
- Synthesise the responses — where is there alignment? Where are the gaps and conflicts?

**Week 3-4: Current State Assessment**
- Map the end-to-end customer journey today: touchpoints, pain points, satisfaction scores
- Identify the top 3-5 most painful / most impactful customer experience problems
- Quantify where possible: how many customers are affected? What's the cost of the current experience?

**Week 5-6: Prioritisation and Scope**
- Facilitate a steering committee alignment workshop armed with the data
- Present the top opportunities with a draft business case for each
- Use a value vs effort matrix to prioritise
- Get committee sign-off on scope and success metrics before any development begins

This is the most valuable thing a Senior BA can do on an ambiguous programme — create the clarity that allows everyone else to deliver.

**Scenario 6: Product vs Project — BA in a Product Organisation**

*"Your company is shifting from project-based delivery to product teams. How does your role as BA change?"*

**Answer:**
The skills remain the same but the rhythm and structure change completely. In a product model:
- There are no "projects" with start and end — the product team is permanent
- The product roadmap replaces the project scope document
- Backlog refinement replaces requirements workshops (it's continuous, not a phase)
- The BA works shoulder-to-shoulder with the Product Manager — often the roles partially merge
- Requirements are "just-in-time" (detailed only when a story is about to be developed) not "just-in-case" (fully documented before development starts)
- Success is measured by product metrics (DAU, NPS, retention) not delivery milestones

The Senior BA adds value by: maintaining the long-term vision alongside the immediate sprint, ensuring product decisions are grounded in business strategy (not just user feedback), tracking benefit realisation on shipped features, and challenging the product team when user research pulls in a direction that conflicts with business objectives.

**Scenario 7: Data Quality Issues Blocking the Project**

*"You're three months into a CRM migration project. Discovery reveals that 40% of the existing customer data is incomplete, duplicated, or incorrect. The business assumed the old data would migrate cleanly. What do you do?"*

**Answer:**
Raise the flag immediately — this is a project-level risk that affects timeline, cost, and value.

**Immediate actions:**
1. Quantify the issue: run a data quality assessment with specific dimensions (completeness, accuracy, duplicates, format compliance) per data entity
2. Present findings to PM and sponsor with concrete examples: "40,000 of 100,000 customer records are missing email addresses. Our new marketing module requires email. Without email, these customers cannot be contacted digitally."
3. Assess options:
   - **Cleanse before migration**: Manual or automated data cleansing — how long, how much?
   - **Migrate as-is with flagging**: Migrate dirty data with a "data quality" flag; cleanse post-migration (risk: dirty data in the new system from day 1)
   - **Selective migration**: Only migrate records that meet data quality standards; manage the rest manually
   - **Delayed go-live**: Extend timeline for proper cleansing
4. Quantify the business impact of each option
5. Get the sponsor to make an informed decision — this is not a technical call, it's a business call
6. Post-project: establish data governance to prevent recurrence (data quality SLAs, data stewards, regular quality audits)

**Scenario 8: Managing Multiple Competing Priorities Across Programmes**

*"You are the Senior BA supporting three concurrent programmes. All three programme managers say their work is highest priority. How do you manage this?"*

**Answer:**
This is fundamentally a governance and resource management problem that I surface rather than absorb. First, be transparent: "I have a conflict that needs resolution from a higher level." Present the situation to the Portfolio Manager or PMO: "I have concurrent demands from Programme A (X hours/week), Programme B (Y hours/week), Programme C (Z hours/week). My total capacity is N hours/week. Please help me prioritise."

In parallel, I would: (a) be honest with all three PMs about my constraints — never over-promise and under-deliver; (b) identify quick wins I can deliver to each programme to maintain momentum while the prioritisation is resolved; (c) identify tasks that could be delegated to junior BAs with my oversight; (d) assess which programmes have the highest strategic priority based on objective criteria (business case value, executive visibility, delivery risk) and present that analysis to support the prioritisation decision.

The wrong answer is to silently accept all demands and deliver poor quality across all three. Transparency is the professional response.

### Technical Domain Questions for Senior BAs

**Q7. How do you work with APIs as a BA?**
At the senior level, BAs need API literacy. I can't write API code, but I need to:
- Understand REST principles: endpoints, HTTP methods (GET, POST, PUT, DELETE), request/response structure
- Read and interpret API documentation and Swagger/OpenAPI specs
- Define integration requirements: what data needs to flow between systems, in what format, at what frequency, with what error handling
- Specify API contracts: what fields are mandatory, what validation rules apply, what error codes mean
- Understand authentication (API keys, OAuth, JWT) well enough to define security requirements
- Review and sign off on integration design documents

I would never design the API itself — that's the architect/developer's role. But I ensure the API delivers what the business needs.

**Q8. How do you approach requirements for an AI/ML-based solution?**
AI/ML requirements have unique challenges:
- **Outcomes, not instructions**: Define what the model must achieve (e.g., "predict customer churn 30 days in advance with 85% accuracy") not how it works
- **Training data requirements**: What data is needed to train the model? Quality, quantity, labelling
- **Bias and fairness requirements**: Ensure the model doesn't discriminate by protected characteristics
- **Explainability requirements**: In regulated industries, you often need the model to explain its decisions — black-box models may not be acceptable
- **Feedback loop requirements**: How does the model learn from new data? How are corrections made?
- **Monitoring requirements**: What metrics indicate the model is performing correctly? What triggers a review or retrain?
- **Human-in-the-loop**: For high-stakes decisions (loan approvals, medical diagnoses), what is the human review process?

**Q9. How do you manage requirements for regulatory/compliance projects?**
Regulatory projects require a different approach:
- **Start with the regulation**: Read the actual regulation, not a summary. Understand what's mandatory vs guidance.
- **Get a legal/compliance SME**: I am not a lawyer. A compliance SME must validate my requirement interpretation.
- **Compliance traceability**: Every regulation clause must map to at least one requirement. Use a compliance traceability matrix (regulation article → requirement → test case → evidence).
- **Interpretation decisions**: Regulations are often ambiguous. Document each interpretation decision with legal sign-off.
- **Audit trail requirements**: Regulators want evidence of compliance — build in logging, reporting, and evidence collection from day one.
- **Change monitoring**: Regulations change. Build a process for monitoring regulatory updates and assessing their impact on the system.

**Q10. What is the difference between a product roadmap and a release plan?**
A **product roadmap** is strategic: 12-24 month view of what capabilities will be built and why, aligned to product vision and business strategy. It's approximate (themes and outcomes, not detailed features). Audience: executives, investors, sales.

A **release plan** is tactical: sprint-by-sprint delivery plan showing which user stories ship in which release. It's precise (story points, teams, dates). Audience: development teams, project managers, operations.

The Senior BA bridges these: translating roadmap themes into detailed release plans, and ensuring the release plan delivers on the roadmap's strategic intent.
